Do Club Remixes Actually Hit Harder Than the Original?

Post 01 · Track Record · Dataset: Spotify audio features (TidyTuesday, 2020)

You know the moment: a song you already like comes back six months later as a "Remix," suddenly louder, faster, more built for a dance floor. It feels more intense. But is that actually true, according to Spotify's own numbers — or is it just louder mixing and a new drop?

The dataset

This uses Spotify's audio-feature data (danceability, energy, tempo, valence, etc.) as published in the TidyTuesday "Spotify Songs" dataset (2020-01-21), originally pulled via the Spotify API and released under a public/CC0 license. The full dataset has 32,833 tracks.

For this post I worked from a 40-track sample pulled from three Spotify "Pop Remix," "Dance Pop," and "Dance Room" playlists — chosen specifically because several songs show up in both their original and remixed form, which is what makes a real comparison possible. The full working data and code for this post are in the Track Record GitHub repo, or grab the full 32,833-track dataset from the link above to run your own version.

The method

Using Python (pandas + matplotlib):

  1. Flagged any track with "Remix" in its title.
  2. Stripped remix suffixes to recover a "base title," then grouped by artist + base title to find songs with both an original and at least one remix in the sample.
  3. Compared Spotify's energy score (0–1, how intense/fast a track feels) between each matched pair.
  4. Charted the shift per song (slope chart) and the overall group averages across all 40 tracks.

5 of the 7 matched pairs got a higher energy score in remix form — sometimes by a lot (Post Malone/Sam Feldt jumped +0.26). But two went the other way: Kaskade's "With You" is actually calmer as the LöKii remix than the original. Across the full 40-track sample, remixes averaged 0.840 energy vs. 0.763 for originals — a real but not universal bump.

Takeaway: "Remix" leans toward more energy, but it's a trend, not a rule — about 1 in 4 matched remixes in this sample actually dialed things down. And with only 7 matched pairs, this is a fun signal, not a verdict — the full 32,833-track dataset would be the place to check if this holds at scale.
